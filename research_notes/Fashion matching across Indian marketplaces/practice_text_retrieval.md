# Fashion matching across Indian marketplaces: industry practice, text/attribute methods, candidate retrieval

Research date: 2026-11-12. Sources run from 2016 to 2026; I prefer 2020 to 2026 and flag older ones. "VENDOR CLAIM" marks marketing figures that nobody has audited. "VERIFIED" marks things I tested myself with low-volume plain HTTP on 2026-11-12, or numbers that come from a peer-reviewed or arXiv method paper.

## (a) How price-intelligence and catalogue teams match apparel and footwear across retailers, and what match rates they report

### Takeaway
Vendors agree that fashion is the hardest category. DataWeave's own blog (June 2026) puts typical apparel match rates at 40–60%, against about 95% for electronics. Every vendor combines identifiers (where they exist), attribute extraction, image embeddings and human review, and sorts results into tiers (exact / variant / similar). The one well-documented industrial fashion matcher, from Zalando (2024), finds that images do most of the work and that text on its own is weak. It also needs expert human-in-the-loop review to reach about 90% precision.

### Cited Findings
**DataWeave**
- VENDOR CLAIM (blog "From Mess To Match", Sadananda Vaidya, 1 Jun 2026): "In apparel, match rates typically sit between 40% and 60%, compared to 95% in electronics." Reasons given: similarity is subjective (style, colour, gender, fabric, texture), fast-fashion churn, size charts that vary by brand, country and style, sets versus separates, and third-party seller noise. — [DataWeave blog](https://dataweave.com/blog/from-mess-to-match-solving-product-identity-at-retail-scale)
- Same post: LLMs handle text matching and pull attributes out of messy descriptions. CLIP embeddings, Siamese networks and CNN global features handle images. Fashion attributes extracted are neck type, pattern, sleeve length, pocket presence and fabric composition. About 90% of matches are processed automatically and "the remaining edge cases route to specialist human reviewers". Claimed results: more than 15% accuracy gain over baseline in fashion after adding LLM and CLIP, precision above 85% in fashion and electronics, and attribute tagging accuracy above 75% for the top five categories. — [DataWeave blog](https://dataweave.com/blog/from-mess-to-match-solving-product-identity-at-retail-scale)
- Tiers in the same post: **Exact** (same product across retailers, mostly where identifiers are strong) and **Similar** (shares key attributes; "fixed" attributes such as category plus "flexible" ones such as price band; described as critical for private label and fashion). It also lists variants, substitutes, competitor, basket and counterfeit matches. The word "equivalent" does not appear. — [DataWeave blog](https://dataweave.com/blog/from-mess-to-match-solving-product-identity-at-retail-scale)
- VENDOR CLAIM (fashion e-commerce case study, undated, probably October of an unstated year): a US fashion retailer monitoring 200,000 SKUs daily got a 75% match rate on high-priority products, "surpassing the fashion industry benchmark of 50%". The accuracy claim is "99%+" on the HTML page, but a search snippet of the PDF version said "95%+", so the two versions of the same case study disagree. No method is described. — [DataWeave case study page](https://dataweave.com/pages/using-dataweaves-ai-powered-pricing-intelligence-for-fashion-ecommerce-retailer); [PDF](https://dataweave.com/downloads/leading-fashion-ecommerce-retailer-pricing-intelligence-case-study-dataweave.pdf)
- VENDOR CLAIM (third-party case-study aggregator): Adidas/Reebok LATAM, about 60% of roughly 6,000 SKUs matched across 24 retailers within a month, ">95% accuracy". — [casestudies.com](https://www.casestudies.com/company/dataweave/case-study/adidas-customer-case-study)
- DataWeave colour and size normalisation (Joe Bunner, 6 Nov 2024): match first on identifiers (UPC, SKU code, other attributes), then use LLM prompts to normalise size and colour to a standard (for example "Sm" to "S"), "combined with human verification". It cites one retailer with more than 1,000 distinct values for shirts and tops. No explicit match-definition rule is given. — [DataWeave blog](https://dataweave.com/blog/normalizing-size-and-color-in-fashion-using-ai-to-power-competitive-price-intelligence)
- DataWeave also uses Siamese networks on images to label pairs "Exact Match", and its Véracité human-in-the-loop product adds human review where criteria are aesthetic (fashion). — [DataWeave Siamese blog](https://dataweave.com/blog/using-siamese-networks-to-power-accurate-product-matching-in-ecommerce); [Véracité](https://dataweave.com/in/veracite)

**Intelligence Node**
- VENDOR CLAIM: three match types, **exact** (like-to-like branded), **variant**, and **similar** (private label). "99% accuracy". Its fashion similar-match process compares attributes, keeps candidates within a similar price range, and uses computer vision to confirm a visual match. Dress examples list style, design, colour, hemline, sleeve type and material. Its blog says missing unique identifiers are mainly a problem in Fashion, Home and Beauty. — [Intelligence Node product matching](https://www.intelligencenode.com/blog/product-matching-ai-for-ecommerce/); [What makes us different](https://www.intelligencenode.com/what-makes-us-different/); [2022 guide PDF](https://info.intelligencenode.com/hubfs/ebooks/2022/A-Guide-to-AI-driven-Product-Matching-2022.pdf); [apparel page](https://info.intelligencenode.com/apparel-and-fashion)

**Prisync / Price2Spy / Competera** (all summarised from search snippets of vendor pages; low detail)
- Prisync: exact matching is driven by GTIN/UPC/EAN, and it tracks variant prices across sizes and colours shown as rows, drop-downs or buttons. A third-party review calls its matching essentially a barcode lookup. — [Prisync variant tracking](https://prisync.com/features/product-variant-price-tracking/); [Prisync matching](https://prisync.com/features/product-matching/); [prowebscraper comparison](https://prowebscraper.com/articles/product-matching-tools-comparison)
- Price2Spy: treats each variant on a shared page as a separate product and advises matching at the most specific relevant level (colour, size). ML "Hybrid" matching is Enterprise-tier only. — [Price2Spy product matching](https://www.price2spy.com/en/pricing/product-matching.html); [Price2Spy blog](https://www.price2spy.com/blog/?p=12445)
- Competera: offers "similar matching" (use-case PDF). A third-party comparison cites reviewer complaints about missed easy matches. — [Competera similar matching PDF](https://competera.net/pdf/Use_Case_CD_Similar_Matching_en_fin.pdf); [prowebscraper comparison](https://prowebscraper.com/articles/product-matching-tools-comparison)
- Octaprice (fashion-focused): claims its AI identifies the same item "regardless of colour, size, or regional sizing convention". — [Octaprice fashion](https://www.octaprice.com/industries/fashion)

**Zalando (best-documented industrial fashion matcher)**
- Tóth et al., "End-to-end multi-modal product matching in fashion e-commerce", arXiv 2403.11593 (Mar 2024). Data: about 2M offers across 5 domains (sellers or stores). Over 99% of index offers have no match in the query set. A match means the same ground-truth product ID. Model: frozen CLIP ViT-bigG-14 image encoder (averaged over the offer's images) plus CLIP text encoder over brand and title plus 3 numeric features (for example number of sizes, original price), with a linear projection to 192 dimensions trained with supervised contrastive loss. Training takes about 10 minutes on one A100. — [arXiv 2403.11593](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.11593v1)
- VERIFIED (paper numbers), AUCPR / R@1, in-domain: **text-only 0.6 / 11.1**, image-only 57.2 / 77.8, multimodal 66.1 / 84.2 (R@3 95.2). Out-domain: text-only 2.6 / 17.9, multimodal 63.3 / 82.1. The authors conclude that visual information is the most important modality in fashion. — [arXiv 2403.11593](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.11593v1)
- Production: candidates are blocked by **fuzzy brand-name similarity** before nearest-neighbour search, then a cosine-distance threshold is tuned on PR curves. Human-in-the-loop: validators see the top 3 neighbours and pick the match or none. Crowd workers (even with 5 votes) did not reach the required 90% precision, so expert workers were used. HITL output precision was 0.896 (from model-input precision 0.162) and 0.937 (from 0.285). Colour was hidden from validators because showing it did not improve validation. — [arXiv 2403.11593](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.11593v1)
- Zalando's partner onboarding uses EAN as a mandatory attribute for article mapping (identifier-first where possible). — [Zalando partner university](https://partner.zalando.com/university/article/onboarding-stage-2-article-mapping)

**Amazon**
- MAPS: Multimodal Attention for Product Similarity (Das et al., WACV 2022) targets catalogue variation errors, **Duplicate Variation** and **Inconsistent Variation**, using weakly supervised multimodal (image plus title) metric learning on raw catalogue data. — [MAPS paper](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/WACV2022/papers/Das_MAPS_Multimodal_Attention_for_Product_Similarity_WACV_2022_paper.pdf); [Amazon Science blog](https://amazon.science/blog/using-computer-vision-to-weed-out-product-catalogue-errors)
- An Amazon Science paper titled "Using brand knowledge bases and LLM agents to enhance e-commerce retailers' catalog quality" exists (it appeared in search results). The PDF link returned 404 when I fetched it, so I could not check its contents. — [Amazon Science PDF (404 on fetch)](https://assets.amazon.science/94/50/2e467c6c4fe79c4cbfc358b62d8a/scipub-approval152129-41682263-using-brand-knowledge-bases-and-llm-agents-to-enhance-ecommerce-retailers-catalog-quality.pdf)

**Flipkart / Myntra (India)**
- Flipkart's 2017 visual search paper (arXiv 1703.02344, older than my preferred range) notes that multiple sellers upload the same product, "intentionally to increase their visibility, or unintentionally". It is about visual recommendation and search, not dedup. — [arXiv 1703.02344](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.02344)
- Myntra: the papers I found are on similar-product recommendation (nearest neighbours within an article type, cosine distance), "Buy Me That Look" visual retrieval, and Attr2Style (AAAI 2021, piloted at Myntra; infers style from attributes). None is a cross-retailer matching or dedup paper. — [Attr2Style](https://mlanthology.org/aaai/2021/banerjee2021aaai-attr); [arXiv 2008.11638](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.11638.pdf)
- Non-Indian marketplace dedup example: Hepsiburada (Turkey), arXiv 2509.15858 (Sep 2025). A BERT-based text model plus MaskedAutoEncoder image embeddings feed a vector search for duplicates. — [arXiv 2509.15858](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.15858v2)

### Inferences
- The "40–60% apparel vs 95% electronics" figure exists, but its only source is DataWeave's own blog (2026), and it is a coverage (match-rate) number, not precision. Vendors separately claim 85–99% precision on what they do match. "Match rate" and "accuracy" are never defined consistently, so the two numbers should not be combined.
- Practitioners trade coverage for precision with tiers (exact, then variant, then similar) and human review on the uncertain band. Zalando's numbers show that even a strong multimodal model gives low raw precision on its top-k candidates (0.16–0.29), and expert review lifts it to about 0.9.
- Zalando's text-only result (AUCPR 0.6) is a warning for a text-first pipeline. Titles that each retailer writes differently carry little identity signal on their own. Text is most useful for **blocking (brand) and attribute constraints (colour, gender, category)**, while identity confirmation needs images, a style code or EAN, or a canonical name.

### Gaps
- I found no published Lyst or Farfetch technical write-up on cross-boutique matching. The only Lyst claim found (graph neural networks) is from an unsourced aggregator and is excluded.
- I found no Wiser, Edited, Increff or Actowiz fashion match-rate figures (these were not searched in depth because of the tool budget).
- I found no Flipkart- or Myntra-authored paper on cross-seller "same product" clustering or dedup. Two USPTO patents titled "System and method for managing product catalogs" (10210553, 10796352) describe grouping duplicates, but I did not confirm the assignee.
- No vendor uses the exact tier label "equivalent". Intelligence Node uses "variant" and DataWeave uses "similar".

## (b) Text and attribute techniques for coping with titles that each retailer writes differently (the Puma 52885601 case)

### Takeaway
The workable text strategy is to normalise each listing into a structured key: brand + product line/model + gender + category + colour, with style code or EAN where available. It is not to compare raw titles. For the Puma case, **the brand site acts as a verified bridge**. in.puma.com resolves style 528856 to the canonical name "Train All Day Men's Breathable Training Tee". Its URL slug contains "train-all-day-essential", which links Amazon's model name "TAD ESSENTIAL" to Myntra's title. It also exposes EAN-13 barcodes per variant. LLMs are a reasonable choice for attribute extraction and normalisation. Published results put GPT-4-class models at about 91% F1 for title attribute extraction (general products), while zero-shot fashion attribute accuracy is much weaker.

### Cited Findings
**Brand-site bridge, VERIFIED 2026-11-12 with plain curl (one request each, no bot protection hit):**
- `https://in.puma.com/in/en/search?q=528856` returned HTTP 200 with results titled "Train All Day Men's Breathable Training Tee" in several colourways. It listed variant IDs 528856_01, _02, _18, _35, _46, _73, _75, _99. — [in.puma.com search](https://in.puma.com/in/en/search?q=528856)
- The product page `https://in.puma.com/in/en/pd/train-all-day-essential-mens-t-shirt/528856?swatch=01` has the **slug "train-all-day-essential-mens-t-shirt"** and display name "Train All Day Men's Breathable Training Tee". Embedded data shows `masterId "528856"`, colorValue "01" = **"PUMA Black"**, gender "Male", material "Shell: 100% Polyester", price 1799. So 52885601 = style 528856 + colour 01 (PUMA Black). — [in.puma.com product page](https://in.puma.com/in/en/pd/train-all-day-essential-mens-t-shirt/528856?swatch=01)
- The same page's embedded data contains 13-digit `variantId` values such as 4069159954134, 4069159954202, 4069159954271, 4069159954349 and 4069159954417 (one per size). These look like EAN-13/GTIN barcodes (my inference from the format; not confirmed by Puma). — [in.puma.com product page](https://in.puma.com/in/en/pd/train-all-day-essential-mens-t-shirt/528856?swatch=01)
- The title variants from the brief line up with this: Myntra's "Puma Train All Day Men Breathable Training Tee" is nearly verbatim Puma's own display name. Amazon's "TAD ESSENTIAL" is the abbreviation of the slug's "train-all-day-essential". Amazon's "Polyester" and Flipkart's "Black" / "Men" match Puma's material, colour and gender fields. (Comparison of the above source against the brief's titles.)

**Style-code lookup on marketplaces, VERIFIED 2026-11-12 (negative results):**
- `https://www.myntra.com/528856` returned a **different product**, "Blackberrys Men Khaki Sharp Fit Trousers … 528856". Myntra's numeric URL path is Myntra's own style ID, not the brand's article number, so a numeric brand code can collide with an unrelated Myntra product. — [myntra.com/528856](https://www.myntra.com/528856)
- `https://www.flipkart.com/search?q=puma+528856` returned HTTP 200, but the results were unrelated Puma shoes (sneakers, running shoes), with no Train All Day tee in the parsed product links. **Attribute extraction and normalisation**
- DataWeave extracts neck type, pattern, sleeve length, pocket presence and fabric composition for fashion, and uses LLM prompts to normalise colour and size. — [DataWeave 2026](https://dataweave.com/blog/from-mess-to-match-solving-product-identity-at-retail-scale); [DataWeave 2024](https://dataweave.com/blog/normalizing-size-and-color-in-fashion-using-ai-to-power-competitive-price-intelligence)
- Brinkmann, Shraga and Bizer (arXiv 2403.02130, Mar 2024): GPT-4 extracts and normalises attribute values from product titles and descriptions at **F1 91%**, beating pretrained-language-model extractors by about 10%. Dataset is WDC PAVE (general products, not fashion-only). — [arXiv 2403.02130](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.02130v1)
- Zero-shot fine-grained fashion attributes from images (arXiv 2507.09950, 2025): Gemini 2.0 Flash macro-F1 56.79% vs GPT-4o-mini 43.28%. Fine-grained fashion attributes are still hard for off-the-shelf LLMs. — [arXiv 2507.09950](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09950v2)
- Classic title sequence labelling (Walmart, More 2016, older): CRF-style NER on titles, noting that titles have no fixed syntax. — [arXiv 1608.04670](https://arxiv.org/html/1608.04670)

**LLM entity matching (text pairs)**
- Peeters, Steiner and Bizer, "Entity Matching using Large Language Models" (arXiv 2310.11244, v4 Oct 2024): zero-shot GPT-4 can beat PLM matchers fine-tuned on large training sets and is more robust on unseen entities. Evaluated on WDC Products, which has 80% corner cases. — [arXiv 2310.11244](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.11244)
- Steiner, Peeters and Bizer, "Fine-tuning LLMs for Entity Matching" (arXiv 2409.08185, 2024): fine-tuning GPT-4o and Llama-3.1, including with generated explanations. The F1 of about 87 for fine-tuned GPT-4o on WDC Products is from an aggregator and was not checked in the paper. — [arXiv 2409.08185](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.08185v2)
- WDC Products benchmark (Peeters, Der, Bizer, arXiv 2301.09521, 2023): all systems struggle on unseen entities. — [arXiv 2301.09521](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09521v2)

**Generic or system-made titles (Flipkart)**
- A Flipkart seller catalogue guide (Scribd copy, undated) says the product title as shown on the site "can't be edited by you" (the seller). This suggests the platform controls it. — [Scribd Flipkart guide](https://www.scribd.com/document/636096254/Untitled)
- Flipkart filters run on structured attributes (fabric, fit, sleeve length, pattern, occasion). Missing attributes drop a listing from filtered results. — [base.com Flipkart audit guide](https://base.com/en-IN/blog/?p=20951)
- Analogue: noon automatically appends the "color name" attribute to apparel titles. — [noon partner help](https://helpcenter.noon.partners/en/category/product-listing/title-requirements-and-rejection-reasons-for-the-seller-sku)

### Inferences
- Flipkart titles like "PUMA Printed Men Round Neck Black T-Shirt" are best treated as a **bag of attributes** (brand, pattern, gender, neck, colour, category) to use as **hard or soft constraints**, not as identity evidence. Note that "Printed" (Flipkart) vs "Solid" (Amazon) for the same item shows that pattern attributes on marketplaces are noisy. They should be soft constraints or ignored, not hard filters.
- A practical normalisation pipeline: (1) parse brand, gender, category, colour; (2) map the product line through a brand line dictionary with abbreviations (TAD → Train All Day, ESS → Essentials, and so on), seeded from the brand site's names and slugs; (3) resolve the style code against the brand site to get the canonical name, colour name ("PUMA Black") and EANs; (4) use the canonical name for marketplace queries and for LLM pairwise verification. Steps 2–4 are design inferences supported by the verified Puma bridge above, not a documented vendor practice.
- An LLM can propose abbreviation expansions ("TAD ESSENTIAL" → "Train All Day Essential"), but the brand-site slug gives a verifiable ground truth. Prefer lookup over generation where the brand site is reachable, and use the LLM as a fallback with verification.
- EANs from the brand site could give exact matches where a marketplace exposes a barcode (Amazon sometimes lists EAN or "Item model number"). I did not verify whether the Amazon.in or Flipkart pages for this item expose EANs.

### Gaps
- I did not verify whether Amazon.in exposes EAN/GTIN or the Puma article number (52885601) in its product detail section for this listing.
- I found no public Puma "product line abbreviation" dictionary. A TAD = Train All Day dictionary would have to be built from brand-site names and slugs.
- I found no published Flipkart document describing the title-generation template for fashion.
- I found no published accuracy figures for LLM matching of Indian fashion listings specifically.

## (c) Candidate retrieval when site search ranks the true item low, and what "same product" means in fashion practice

### Takeaway
The literature and practitioners treat candidate retrieval as a separate stage whose recall caps everything downstream. Industrial systems block on brand and then run embedding nearest-neighbour search over **their own crawled catalogue index**, rather than relying on a marketplace's search ranking. For live-search pipelines, the evidence points to these steps:
1. Build queries from the canonical brand-site name.
2. Run several query variants.
3. Fetch deeper result pages (at least 20–40), given the observed ranks of 14 and 7.
4. Rerank candidates locally with attributes, images and an LLM.

"Same product" in practice means the same ground-truth style. Colourway is usually part of identity for price intelligence, and size is handled as a variant.

### Cited Findings
- Zalando blocks with fuzzy brand-name similarity, then runs kNN on precomputed embeddings within similar brands. R@1 is 84.2 but **R@3 is 95.2**, so the true match often sits just below rank 1 even in a good system. Validators review the top 3. — [arXiv 2403.11593](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.11593v1)
- Practitioner guidance: evaluate candidate retrieval separately from final ranking, because a correct product missed at retrieval cannot be recovered later. — [HackerNoon](https://hackernoon.com/why-your-llm-keeps-matching-the-wrong-products-and-why-a-better-prompt-wont-fix-it)
- LLM query expansion for product retrieval: TREC 2025 Product Search (JBNU) used an LLM to expand product queries into Lucene syntax for BM25, plus ColBERTv2 or SPLADE candidate pools. Alibaba (arXiv 2602.23620) generates several LLM query rewrites. A naive free-form synthetic query generator reportedly scored below 50% precision/recall. — [TREC JBNU](https://trec.nist.gov/pubs/trec34/papers/JBNU.product.pdf); [arXiv 2602.23620](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.23620v1); [Towards AI via scour](https://scour.ing/p/https://pub.towardsai.net/solving-the-cold-start-problem-in-e-commerce-search-with-llms-f2c0f54f7e96)
- Style-code search on Indian marketplaces fails here (VERIFIED above): Flipkart's "puma 528856" returned unrelated shoes, and myntra.com/528856 maps to a different product (Myntra's internal ID space). — [Flipkart search](https://www.flipkart.com/search?q=puma+528856); [myntra.com/528856](https://www.myntra.com/528856)
- The brand-site lookup by style code works for Puma India (VERIFIED). It gives the canonical name, colour name and gender to build marketplace queries ("puma train all day breathable training tee black men"). — [in.puma.com search](https://in.puma.com/in/en/search?q=528856)
- **Definition of "same product"**: Zalando defines a match as the same ground-truth product ID and found that showing colour to validators did not help. Price2Spy advises matching at the most specific variant level (colour, size). Prisync and Price2Spy track size and colour as variants. DataWeave normalises size and colour to compare at variant level, and treats colour and size price gaps as real (a women's shirt at ₹389 in S vs ₹399 in XL). — [arXiv 2403.11593](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.11593v1); [Price2Spy blog](https://www.price2spy.com/blog/?p=12445); [Prisync variants](https://prisync.com/features/product-variant-price-tracking/); [DataWeave 2024](https://dataweave.com/blog/normalizing-size-and-color-in-fashion-using-ai-to-power-competitive-price-intelligence)
- Intelligence Node restricts similar-match candidates to a similar price range, so price acts as a retrieval and filter constraint. Zalando feeds price and number of sizes in as model features. — [Intelligence Node](https://www.intelligencenode.com/blog/product-matching-ai-for-ecommerce/); [arXiv 2403.11593](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.11593v1)

### Inferences
- The observed ranks (14th on Flipkart, 7th on Myntra) mean a top-5 or top-10 cutoff would miss the item. A fetch depth of about 40 results (one or two pages), across 2–4 query variants (canonical name; model name plus colour; Amazon-style attribute string; the abbreviation expanded and not expanded), is a reasonable recall-first setting. Dedupe the union, then rerank locally with brand, gender, category and colour hard filters, name-token overlap with the canonical name, image similarity, and an LLM pairwise judge.
- Web search (`site:myntra.com "train all day" puma tee`) is an untested fallback. I did not run Google site: queries (to avoid automated scraping of Google), and found no source measuring its recall for Indian marketplaces.
- For a price-intelligence "same product" definition: same brand + style code (or canonical name) + colourway + gender. Size is ignored for identity but captured for price and availability. Tiers: **exact** (style code, EAN or canonical name plus colour confirmed), **variant** (same style, different colour), **similar** (attribute match only). This mirrors Intelligence Node and DataWeave tiering.
- Matching the colour name needs normalisation: Puma's "PUMA Black" vs marketplaces' "Black". Puma colour code "01" = PUMA Black for this style.

### Gaps
- I did not test Myntra or Flipkart search with the canonical-name query to measure the rank improvement over the original queries (not done, to keep marketplace requests minimal). This is the obvious next experiment.
- Image-search retrieval (Google Lens, marketplace visual search) for Indian marketplaces: no measured source found.
- I did not test whether other brands' Indian sites (Nike, Adidas, H&M, etc.) support style-code search the way in.puma.com does. The bridge is verified only for Puma.
