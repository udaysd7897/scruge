# Industry practice: cross-retailer product matching (with India focus)

Research date: 2026-11-11. About 25 search and fetch calls. Medium-hosted engineering blogs (idealo, PriceRunner, Walmart Labs) returned HTTP 403, so details for those come from search snippets and conference abstracts, not the full posts. Vendor accuracy claims are marked as marketing unless noted otherwise.

## Q1. How do comparison engines (Google Shopping, PriceRunner, Idealo, Keepa, CamelCamelCamel, Indian comparators) match the same product across stores?

### Takeaway
Two broad models are in use. Google Shopping relies on merchants supplying identifiers (GTIN, or brand + MPN) and groups offers that share a GTIN. European comparators that ingest messy shop feeds (idealo, PriceRunner) have published ML designs: metric learning or embeddings (triplet or siamese networks, Sentence-Transformers plus Faiss kNN) that map incoming offers to an existing catalogue product, and cluster offers that fail to map into new products. I found no published engineering detail for the Indian comparators (BuyHatke, Smartprix, 91mobiles, MySmartPrice, PriceHistory.app). Their public pages show that they keep a canonical spec catalogue keyed on model and RAM/storage variant, then attach retailer offers to it.

### Cited Findings
- **Google identifiers (official).** Google says each product and each variant (different colour or size) has its own GTIN. Without a GTIN, "accurate matching to products can't be assured" and visibility may be limited. Private-label or store-brand items without a GTIN should submit brand + MPN. A GTIN is "ideally provided in combination with MPN." — [Google Merchant Center: Use the GTIN](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6324461)
- **Google multipacks and bundles (official).** For a manufacturer-made multipack or bundle, submit the identifiers of the pack or bundle. For a merchant-made one, submit the identifiers of the individual or main product and flag it with `is_bundle` or `multipack`. GTINs must have a valid check digit, and up to 10 GTINs can be given per product. — [Google Merchant Center: Use the GTIN](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6324461)
- **Google variants (official).** Products that share a GTIN but should be variants (size or colour) trigger a "Duplicate GTIN" issue. They should be linked with `item_group_id` and given distinct GTINs. Offers that share a GTIN must have consistent attributes (brand and so on). — [Google: How to fix Duplicate GTIN](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/12470642); [Google: Same GTIN but differing value](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/12473099)
- **Google clustering mechanics.** Google gives no official description of how its clustering algorithm groups offers into one product card. Third-party feed vendors say offers with the same GTIN are put on a shared product card and that Google looks up GTINs in its Knowledge Graph. These are unverified marketing claims. — [Webtoffee GTIN blog](https://www.webtoffee.com/blog/gtin-google-shopping-listings/); [PriceAPI: Google Shopping product ID matching](https://readme.priceapi.com/docs/google-shopping-product-id-matching-process)
- **GS1 UK (2026).** GS1 UK has published guidance on Google's March 2026 changes to GTIN and product ID handling. The same item with a different price, availability or condition online versus in store should be submitted as separate product IDs. — [GS1 UK](https://www.gs1uk.org/insights/news/Google-changes-in-March-2026-how-to-get-GTINs-and-product-IDs-right)
- **idealo (2022).** idealo receives "millions of offers" daily that cannot be mapped to its catalogue. It encodes offers with Sentence-Transformers, runs kNN with Facebook Faiss, and fine-tunes with siamese, triplet and additive angular margin losses. Baselines were probabilistic record linkage and TF-IDF. The goal is to cluster unmatched offers into new catalogue products. — [PyCon DE 2022 talk, Lennan & Wanner (idealo)](https://pycon.de/archive/2022/talks/transformer-based-clustering-identifying-product-clusters-for-e-commerce/); blog post (403, snippet only): [idealo tech blog](https://medium.com/idealo-tech-blog/automate-product-creation-by-clustering-millions-of-shops-offers-with-machine-learning-3db3162dbad4)
- **PriceRunner (June 2021).** PriceRunner treats offer-to-product matching as large-scale instance classification. It uses metric learning with a neural network trained on triplet loss. — [PriceRunner tech blog (snippet; full page 403)](https://medium.com/pricerunner-tech/large-scale-instances-classification-in-the-wild-c0e8ba221f1e)
- **Indian comparators.** Their public pages show canonical variant-level product pages, such as Smartprix "samsung-galaxy-a55-5g-8gb-ram-256gb", MySmartPrice "Samsung Galaxy A17 5G 256GB 8GB RAM", and 91mobiles "samsung-galaxy-m55-5g-256gb-price-in-india". Each page aggregates Amazon, Flipkart, Croma and other offers. Smartprix says it covers Amazon, Flipkart, Samsung, Croma and "100+ online stores." — [Smartprix A55](https://www.smartprix.com/mobiles/samsung-galaxy-a55-5g-8gb-ram-256gb-ppd10l49lwem); [MySmartPrice A17](https://www.mysmartprice.com/mobile/samsung-galaxy-a17-256gb-8gb-ram-msp620220); [91mobiles M55](https://www.91mobiles.com/samsung-galaxy-m55-5g-256gb-price-in-india); [Smartprix app listing](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/smartprix-price-comparison/id1142915507)
- **BuyHatke (2013).** Early coverage describes BuyHatke as "grabbing results from various online shopping portals" for dynamic price comparison. It gives no matching method. — [YourStory 2013](https://yourstory.com/2013/09/buyhatke-iit-kgp-price-discovery-ecommerce)
- **PriceHistory.app.** It keeps per-listing pages for Myntra and Flipkart items and uses retailer titles verbatim, for example "Nike Men Black & Blue Colourblocked Court Vision LO Leather Sneakers". This suggests per-retailer listing tracking rather than heavy cross-store canonicalisation for fashion. — [PriceHistory.app listing](https://pricehistoryapp.com/product/nike-men-black-blue-colourblocked-court-vision-lo-leather-sneakers)

### Inferences
- Keepa and CamelCamelCamel track one retailer (Amazon), keyed by ASIN, so they don't need cross-store matching. I found no source for this in this pass; it is common knowledge, so treat it as unverified here.
- Indian phone comparators almost certainly key on brand + model + (RAM, storage), with colour folded into one page. This is visible in their URL schemes. Fashion matching across Myntra, Ajio and Flipkart is visibly weaker or absent on these sites.
- The common pattern is "map to an existing catalogue entity first, cluster the leftovers to create new entities." A system built for a small set of fixed retailers can start with a curated canonical catalogue plus per-retailer listing links.

- I found no official Google description of its offer-clustering algorithm.
- I could not fetch the full idealo or PriceRunner posts (403), so I have no thresholds or metrics from them.

## Q2. How do price-intelligence vendors describe their matching (identifiers, AI, human QA, exact vs similar)?

### Takeaway
Every major vendor describes the same architecture. Identifiers come first, then text and attribute normalization, then text and image embeddings. Matches are split into "exact" and "similar" (plus "variant"), and confidence-based routing sends high-confidence pairs to auto-accept or auto-reject and the middle band to human QA. The headline accuracy figures (90–99%) are vendor marketing. The most informative vendor detail is DataWeave's admission that apparel match rates are only about 40–60%, against about 95% for electronics.

### Cited Findings
- **DataWeave (India-founded; post dated 1 June 2026): signals.** UPC, GTIN and EAN identifiers are high precision but often missing (retailers omit them, use internal SKUs, or list multipacks with unit codes). Title fuzzy matching is described as "brittle." DataWeave uses transformer embeddings, LLM attribute extraction, image preprocessing (background removal, object detection), CLIP embeddings, Siamese networks for pairwise scoring and CNN features, with over 100M images indexed. — [DataWeave, From Mess to Match (2026)](https://dataweave.com/blog/from-mess-to-match-solving-product-identity-at-retail-scale)
- **DataWeave: match taxonomy.** Exact means the same product across retailers and reaches 95%+ accuracy when identifiers are clean, mainly in electronics. Similar uses "fixed" attributes (must match, e.g. category) and "flexible" attributes with a tolerance (e.g. price band), with tolerances that vary by vertical. Other match types are variants (size or colour of a reference product), substitutes, basket or bundle matches, and counterfeit detection. In home improvement, a 4-inch and a 6-inch fixture are different products, not variants. DataWeave warns that treating exact and similar matching as one problem yields "confidently wrong results." — [DataWeave 2026](https://dataweave.com/blog/from-mess-to-match-solving-product-identity-at-retail-scale)
- **DataWeave: routing and human loop.** About 90% of matches are auto-processed (auto-verified at high confidence or auto-rejected). Edge cases go to specialist reviewers, and every reviewer decision becomes training data. DataWeave reports over 40M verified product pairs. Its human-in-the-loop product is branded "Veracite." — [DataWeave 2026](https://dataweave.com/blog/from-mess-to-match-solving-product-identity-at-retail-scale); [DataWeave Veracite](https://dataweave.com/in/veracite)
- **DataWeave: reported numbers (vendor-reported).** Apparel match rate about 40–60% versus about 95% in electronics. Precision above 85% in electronics and fashion. Attribute-tagging accuracy above 75%. A 15%+ gain after adopting LLMs and CLIP. Elsewhere DataWeave claims "99%+" accuracy, and an older video claims ">90%". These figures are inconsistent, and none is independently verified. — [DataWeave 2026](https://dataweave.com/blog/from-mess-to-match-solving-product-identity-at-retail-scale); [DataWeave product matching page](https://dataweave.com/us/product-matching); [DataWeave video post](https://dataweave.com/blog/e5eb8934704a)
- **DataWeave: other pitfalls.** Private-label titles that mimic brands, generic phrases that match unrelated SKUs, third-party seller price variability, and regional brand-name differences. — [DataWeave 2026](https://dataweave.com/blog/from-mess-to-match-solving-product-identity-at-retail-scale)
- **Intelligence Node (blog dated 29 Sep 2021).** Matching is attribute-based with attribute standardization; their example is "Long Sleeve" versus "Full-Length Sleeve." They say NLP alone is insufficient, so computer vision is added, with a "patented Similarity Engine." Low-confidence matches go to QA specialists. Accuracy is said to be backed by contractual SLAs. — [Intelligence Node blog](https://www.intelligencenode.com/blog/product-matching-ai-for-ecommerce/)
- **Intelligence Node: "99% accuracy" (marketing).** They define the 99% claim as about 1 wrong exact match per 100 SKUs, i.e. a precision or false-positive figure, not recall. Match types are exact (branded like-for-like), similar (private label) and variant. Similar matches are checked by attribute comparison, a price-band restriction and computer-vision confirmation. — [Intelligence Node product matching](https://intelligencenode.com/solutions/product-matching); [Intelligence Node: what makes us different](https://www.intelligencenode.com/what-makes-us-different/)
- **Price2Spy (June 2020 ML series).** Matching about 50K × 40K products gives about 2B candidate pairs. True matches were about 0.002% of pairs, a severe class imbalance. Selecting the best candidates after scoring is framed as bipartite matching. Label noise came from incomplete manual labelling, and the model often found real matches missing from the labels, so PR curves had to be manually re-checked. Duplicate URLs and multi-category listings polluted training pairs. — [Price2Spy Part 3](https://www.price2spy.com/blog/part-3-for-ml-experts-why-is-product-matching-so-difficult/)
- **Price2Spy, later parts.** Snippets indicate that ML accuracy was "above 95%" but human review remains necessary ("ML does work, but it's not magic"). I did not fetch the full posts. — [Price2Spy Part 9](https://price2spy.com/blog/?p=7309); [Price2Spy Part 8](https://www.price2spy.com/blog/part-8-product-matching-via-ml-testing-on-various-industries-languages/)
- **Unnamed "Amazon of India" retailer's pricing-intelligence SaaS (Zilliz case study, undated).** Pipeline: crawl, normalize, proprietary 1024-d e-commerce embeddings in Milvus (DiskANN). Search applies structured pre-filters (category, brand, price range), then vector search, then scoring and a threshold. The page says high-confidence matches go to manual review. Scale: 20M+ client SKUs, 10+ competitors at about 5M SKUs each, and catalog-to-catalog jobs cut from about 12 days to about 2. A legacy FAISS setup needed about 400 GB RAM. — [Zilliz customer story](https://zilliz.com/customers/indian-online-retailer)
- **GroupBWT (consultancy case).** Recommends reporting accuracy per category on held-out data, keeping exact and like-item matching separate, and ending in an analyst dashboard where a person confirms or dismisses each flagged price gap. — [GroupBWT case](https://groupbwt.com/case/development-ai-product-matching-pipelines-for-retail-sector/)
- **Actowiz.** Has a marketing page claiming "99% accuracy in product matching"; I did not fetch it, and its methodology is unknown. — [Actowiz](https://www.actowizsolutions.com/how-actowiz-solutions-achieved-99-accuracy-in-product-matching.php)

### Inferences
- Vendors quote "accuracy" without defining it, or define it as precision on exact matches. Recall (match coverage) is rarely published. The only candid coverage number is DataWeave's 40–60% for apparel. Expect low recall on t-shirts, polos and jerseys, and far better results on phones and TVs.
- The standard pattern for a precision-first system is pre-filter on hard attributes (brand, category, price band), retrieve with embeddings, score, then apply three bands (auto-accept, review, auto-reject). For phones and TVs, hard attributes (RAM, storage, model code, screen size) should be blocking constraints, not soft features.
- Price-band filters help retrieval, but they can hide matches during deep sale events such as Big Billion Days. Use wide bands.

### Gaps
- I did not research Prisync, Competera, PromptCloud, Wiser or Price2Spy's later posts in depth. I have no specifics on their thresholds.
- No vendor publishes numeric confidence thresholds.

## Q3. What do retailer engineering teams (Flipkart, Myntra, Amazon, Walmart, Meesho, etc.) publish?

### Takeaway
The published work is mostly about catalogue deduplication and visual similarity, not competitor matching. The most reusable points are Walmart's (2017) observation that hard negatives differ by a single attribute (storage, colour, condition) and Myntra's research treating colour variants as a distinct visual representation problem.

### Cited Findings
- **Flipkart (2017, arXiv).** Flipkart's visual search and recommendation engine was also used to remove near-duplicate items from the catalogue. The paper says sellers often list the same item independently. — [Flipkart arXiv 1703.02344](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.02344)
- **Flipkart patents.** "Method and system for performing product matching on an e-commerce platform" covers competitive pricing, catalogue dedup and grouping across merchants. A separate patent covers duplicate product detection. I only saw the titles and did not read the claims. — [USPTO 12499478](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12499478); [USPTO 12586108](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12586108)
- **Myntra (2021–22).** Myntra researchers frame colour-variant identification as a visual representation and embedding problem, using self-supervised learning ("A Tale of Color Variants," AAAI 2022). Myntra also holds a patent on determining colour variants of a fashion product. — [arXiv 2112.02910](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02910); [AAAI paper](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/21516); [Patent US10796454B2](https://www.9235.net/patent/US10796454B2.html)
- **Meesho.** Meesho's blog describes kNN over image embeddings for visual similarity of product images. — [Meesho blog](https://meesho.io/blog/predicting-visual-similarities-in-product-images-using-knn)
- **Walmart (2017).** Seller attributes are inconsistent. Random negatives are useless because mismatched items are often near-identical except for one attribute (condition, storage size, colour), so hard-negative mining is needed. — [Walmart Labs: Product matching using deep learning (snippet; page 403)](https://medium.com/walmartlabs/product-matching-in-ecommerce-4f19b6aebaca)
- **Amazon (2021).** Pairwise matching models feed entity resolution, and incremental ER can wrongly merge clusters. Amazon proposes GNNs to detect inconsistent clusters. — [Amazon Science: GNNs for inconsistent cluster detection](https://amazon.science/publications/graph-neural-networks-for-inconsistent-cluster-detection-in-incremental-entity-resolution)
- **Amazon (CorDEL).** A contrastive entity-linkage model. It notes that plain semantic similarity fails on product records where small token differences matter. — [CorDEL PDF](https://cdn.amazon.science/1c/69/487121c7462cacfe59bec851f85d/cordel-a-contrastive-deep-learning-approach-for-entity-linkage.pdf)
- **Turkish marketplace dedup paper (snippet).** Vector distance alone was insufficient because of seller titling and image differences. A pair classifier was trained on 350K labelled pairs. — [chatpaper summary](https://chatpaper.com/paper/190133)

### Inferences
- Use embeddings for recall and an explicit attribute-equality check (or a pair classifier trained on hard negatives) for precision. Transitive clustering needs a consistency check (Amazon's GNN work) so that one bad link does not merge two clusters.

### Gaps
- I found no recent (2023–26) Flipkart or Myntra engineering blog post specifically on competitor or cross-platform matching. I did not search Zalando, eBay or Rakuten in this pass.

## Q4. India-specific listing quirks (smartphones, TVs, sneakers, apparel; availability states)

### Takeaway
Each retailer encodes the same phone or TV differently. Flipkart puts storage in the title and RAM in a separate parenthetical or highlight. Croma URLs and titles use "8gb-ram-256gb-colour". Comparators use "8GB RAM 256GB". TVs carry cm + inch (139 / 139.7 / 138.71 cm all mean 55 inch) plus a model code that is the best key. Sneakers have a manufacturer style code (e.g. Nike HM9862-001) on the brand site, but Myntra and Flipkart titles often use descriptive marketing names ("Black & Blue Colourblocked Court Vision LO") rather than the official colourway.

### Cited Findings
- **Flipkart phone titles.** Format is "Brand Model (Colour, Storage)", with RAM appended or in highlights, e.g. "POCO M7 Pro 5G (Olive Twilight, 256 GB)" and "SAMSUNG Galaxy S24 5G (Blue, Blue, 256 GB)". Highlights show "8 GB RAM | 256 GB ROM", and variant selectors show "256 GB + 8 GB" (per search-result summaries of Flipkart pages). The same itm ID can appear under different colour slugs, e.g. vivo Y300 phantom-purple and titanium-silver both point to itm1f389c323bc6b. — [Flipkart POCO M7 Pro](https://flipkart.com/poco-m7-pro-5g-olive-twilight-256-gb/p/itm1d88319131b56); [Flipkart S24](https://flipkart.com/samsung-galaxy-s24-5g-blue-blue-256-gb/p/itm058d09012b63a); [Flipkart vivo Y300 a](https://www.flipkart.com/vivo-y300-5g-titanium-silver-256-gb/p/itm1f389c323bc6b), [b](https://www.flipkart.com/vivo-y300-5g-phantom-purple-256-gb/p/itm1f389c323bc6b)
- **Croma phone URLs.** Pattern is "brand-model-5g-8gb-ram-256gb-colour-/p/<numeric id>", e.g. samsung-galaxy-a56-5g-8gb-ram-256gb-awesome-lightgray-/p/314441. Croma shows display size in inches and cm, e.g. "6.2 inches (15.64 cm)". — [Croma A56](https://www.croma.com/samsung-galaxy-a56-5g-8gb-ram-256gb-awesome-lightgray-/p/314441); [Croma S24](https://croma.com/samsung-galaxy-s24-5g-8gb-ram-256gb-cobalt-violet-/p/303851)
- **Samsung India official naming.** Uses "Galaxy M55s 5G (8 GB Memory)", with URLs containing the colour, storage and manufacturer SKU (e.g. sm-m558blgcins). The manufacturer SKU encodes the variant, so it is a potential key if retailers expose it. — [Samsung IN M55s](https://www.samsung.com/in/smartphones/galaxy-m/galaxy-m55s-green-256gb-sm-m558blgcins/)
- **Amazon.in phone title format.** Not verified in this pass (no Amazon.in pages returned). Comparators summarise Amazon variants as "8 GB + 256 GB". — [MySmartPrice A17](https://www.mysmartprice.com/mobile/samsung-galaxy-a17-256gb-8gb-ram-msp620220); [FindPrix M35](https://www.findprix.com/mobiles/samsung-galaxy-m35-5g-8gb-ram-256gb-3117)
- **Flipkart TV titles.** Format is "Brand [Series] <cm> cm (<inch> inch) <resolution> <panel> Smart <OS> TV [extras] (<MODEL>)". The cm value varies for the same nominal 55 inch: "139 cm" (LG UQ8020, Blaupunkt, TCL P735), "139.7 cm" (Akai, LG) and "138.71 cm" (iFFALCON). Model numbers appear in the title parenthetical or the spec "Model Name" (55UQ8020PSB, 55CSA7090, 55P735, 55K2A, 55U7H). Titles may include a year ("2025"), long marketing tails, or a warranty ("with 3 years warranty"). One Ridaex listing claimed 4K but listed 1366×768, so attributes can be internally inconsistent. Several listings showed "currently unavailable". — [Flipkart LG UQ8020](https://flipkart.com/lg-uq8020-139-cm-55-inch-ultra-hd-4k-led-smart-webos-tv-2022/p/itmb16e05765d7b2); [Flipkart iFFALCON](https://www.flipkart.com/iffalcon-tcl-led-smart-tv-k2a-android-139-7-cm-55/p/itmf3mcfffcsyxsb); [Flipkart TCL P735](https://flipkart.com/tcl-p735-139-cm-55-inch-ultra-hd-4k-led-smart-google-tv-3-years-warranty/p/itma8a7ad92c5285); [Flipkart Ridaex](https://flipkart.com/ridaex-139-7-cm-55-inch-ultra-hd-4k-led-smart-android-tv/p/itmfgf7kppwzbnh8)
- **Flipkart URL anomaly.** One Flipkart URL slug says "lg-164-cm-65-inch" but resolves to the same itm ID as a 55-inch "139.7 cm" listing. Slugs are unreliable, so parse the page and not the URL. — [Flipkart LG 65 slug](https://www.flipkart.com/lg-164-cm-65-inch-ultra-hd-4k-led-smart-webos-tv/p/itm14880889af054)
- **Sneakers.** Nike India pages show "Colour Shown" (e.g. "Sail/Black/Anthracite/Light Smoke Grey") and "Style" (e.g. IO4468-100, HM9862-001, FJ0685-010). The style code is the most precise key (model + colourway). Nike India URLs sometimes embed the style code. Flipkart Nike listings expose colour but not consistently the style code; one description contained "Style code-833535-110". Myntra-style titles are descriptive ("Nike Men Black & Blue Colourblocked Court Vision LO Leather Sneakers"). nike.in (operated with Nykaa Fashion) uses its own numeric product IDs. — [Nike IN Court Vision Low HM9862-001](https://www.nike.com/in/t/court-vision-low-shoes-M7dKd8/HM9862-001); [Nike IN Next Nature FJ0685-010](https://nike.com/in/t/court-vision-low-next-nature-shoes-bD8HC5/FJ0685-010); [nike.in Court Vision Low](https://www.nike.in/nike-court-vision-low-men-s-shoes/p/24935587); [Flipkart Nike sneaker](https://www.flipkart.com/nike-sneakers-men/p/itma65c6c3c1960f); [PriceHistory.app Myntra listing](https://pricehistoryapp.com/product/nike-men-black-blue-colourblocked-court-vision-lo-leather-sneakers)
- **Apparel attribute synonyms.** For example "Long Sleeve" versus "Full-Length Sleeve" across sites (Intelligence Node), and "denim" versus "jeans" (DataWeave). — [Intelligence Node](https://www.intelligencenode.com/blog/product-matching-ai-for-ecommerce/); [DataWeave 2026](https://dataweave.com/blog/from-mess-to-match-solving-product-identity-at-retail-scale)
- **Availability.** Flipkart and Amazon.in run strong bot protection, especially during festival sales. In-stock status can vary by PIN code. Commercial scrapers model "restocked" and "out_of_stock" events. — [Actowiz guide](https://actowizsolutions.com/flipkart-amazon-meesho-myntra-data-extraction-guide.php); [Apify Flipkart scraper](https://apify.com/parseforge/flipkart-scraper)
- **Restock bots.** PS5 restock bots in India watched Amazon and Flipkart "sold out"/restock states, which shows these states are machine-detectable but page-specific. — [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/news/sony-ps5-india-restock-bot-now-has-flipkart-support)

### Inferences
- **Phones.** Normalise to (brand, model family, 5G flag, RAM_GB, storage_GB, colour). Parse "8 GB RAM | 256 GB ROM", "(8GB RAM, 256GB)", "8GB+256GB" and "256 GB + 8 GB" (on Flipkart, storage comes first in selectors). Treat RAM and storage mismatches as hard negatives (consistent with Walmart's single-attribute hard negatives). Colour may be optional depending on whether prices differ by colour; on Flipkart the price usually does not.
- **TVs.** Key on the manufacturer model code (e.g. 55UQ8020PSB). Strip suffixes cautiously, because LG's 55UQ8020PSB and 55UQ8040PSB are different models. Convert sizes to inches by rounding cm / 2.54 (139, 139.7 and 138.71 all round to 55). Watch for year editions with the same series name.
- **Sneakers.** Key on the style code where available. Otherwise use model name + normalised colour + gender, with low confidence. Myntra-style descriptive colours ("Black & Blue Colourblocked") will not string-match Nike's official colourway names, so image similarity is helpful here.
- **T-shirts, polos, jerseys.** Expect low coverage (DataWeave's 40–60% for apparel). Multi-pack listings ("Pack of 2/3") must be detected and either excluded or normalised per unit; Google's multipack rules show this is a first-class attribute. Size is a variant within one listing, not a separate product. Jerseys need team, season and home/away, and must separate authentic from replica.
- Every link should carry a match status (exact / variant-mismatch / similar / none), and listings should carry an availability status (in stock / out of stock / coming soon / not listed). A missing listing is different from an out-of-stock one.

## Q5. Practical recommendations: precision/recall tradeoffs, thresholds, human review, evaluation

### Takeaway
Industry practice is precision-first for exact matches, with confidence banding: auto-accept the high band, auto-reject the low band, and send the middle to humans (about 10% at DataWeave's scale). Reviewer decisions are fed back as labels. Report metrics per category, separately for exact and similar. Golden sets are hard to build because labels are incomplete: models find real matches that annotators missed.

### Cited Findings
- **Auto-processing rate.** About 90% of matches are auto-verified or auto-rejected; the rest go to human specialists, and their decisions become training data (40M+ verified pairs). — [DataWeave 2026](https://dataweave.com/blog/from-mess-to-match-solving-product-identity-at-retail-scale)
- **Low-confidence QA.** Low-confidence machine matches are verified by QA specialists. — [Intelligence Node](https://www.intelligencenode.com/blog/product-matching-ai-for-ecommerce/)
- **Precision-defined claims.** Intelligence Node's "99%" is a precision-style claim (1 false match per 100). It also lists a separate "recall/matching discovery" metric that is unclear on the page. — [Intelligence Node solutions page](https://intelligencenode.com/solutions/product-matching)
- **Evaluation pitfalls.** Use PR curves for evaluation. Label noise (missed true matches) means apparent false positives must be manually re-checked. The extreme class imbalance (0.002% positives) makes naive accuracy meaningless. Remove duplicate listings before building pairs. — [Price2Spy Part 3](https://www.price2spy.com/blog/part-3-for-ml-experts-why-is-product-matching-so-difficult/)
- **Per-category reporting.** Report accuracy per category on held-out data, keep exact and like-for-like matching separate, and keep a human confirm/dismiss step before acting on price gaps. — [GroupBWT](https://groupbwt.com/case/development-ai-product-matching-pipelines-for-retail-sector/)
- **Staged retrieval.** Hard pre-filters (brand, category, price range), then vector search, then a score threshold, then manual review. — [Zilliz / Indian retailer](https://zilliz.com/customers/indian-online-retailer)
- **Ongoing review.** Price2Spy concludes that ML works (>95% in their tests) but is "not magic", and human review stays in the loop. — [Price2Spy Part 9](https://price2spy.com/blog/?p=7309)

### Inferences
- For a consumer price-comparison product, a false match (showing a 128GB price as the 256GB price) costs more than a missed match. Set the auto-accept threshold for high precision (≥98–99% on a golden set) and show "not found" rather than a guess.
- Build a golden set per category (phones, TVs, sneakers, apparel) with hard negatives: same model with different RAM/storage, same TV series with a different size or year, same shoe with a different colourway, single item versus multi-pack. Measure precision and recall separately per category and per retailer pair.
- Track coverage (the share of canonical products with a found listing per retailer) separately from precision. Missing listings are common and expected, especially on Myntra for electronics or Croma for apparel.
