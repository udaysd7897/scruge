# Gate first, verify last, abstain often

The reliable, general algorithm is the same cascade every large visual-search and offer-matching system uses. The case-by-case rules become five general stages: hard gates on **category, brand and gender**; attribute extraction that reads the **title first** and allows `unknown`; **brand-filtered retrieval** with an optional Google Lens call; **embedding re-ranking** to a shortlist of 3–5; and one **listwise vision-language-model (VLM) check** against an attribute checklist, with a "none" option and abstention. The output is one of four tiers: exact, same style in another colour, similar, or not listed. Google's public documentation shows identity is decided by identifiers wherever they exist. The **variant axes are only colour, size, pattern, material, gender and age group**, so a polo and a crew-neck tee are different products, not variants ([Merchant Center: item_group_id](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6324507)). Lens keeps "Exact matches" and "Visual matches" as separate result sets rather than one ranked list ([SerpApi Lens](https://serpapi.com/google-lens-api)). Each failure in the 10-T-shirt mock run maps onto a stage that was missing. The Tommy watch and the Adidas slides got past because there was no category gate. The round-neck tees got past the Tommy collar check because there was no neckline veto. The Van Heusen and Bewakoof results got past because there was no brand gate. Seller SKUs and colour codes like "LGTGRE" got through because codes were not validated. Unconfirmed Myntra matches came from having no abstention tier. Even the best published fashion matcher is only **16–29% precise** on its raw nearest-neighbour output, so the verifier, not the embedding, decides precision ([Zalando, arXiv 2403.11593](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.11593v1)). Done this way, a lookup costs well under one US cent, plus about $0.004 if Lens is added. Coverage will be partial: vendors report only **40–60% apparel match rates** ([DataWeave](https://dataweave.com/blog/from-mess-to-match-solving-product-identity-at-retail-scale)). The aim is a high-precision "exact" tier, not full coverage.

## Google separates identity from similarity, and so should the app

Google has never published how the Shopping Graph clusters offers that have no identifiers. What it documents is a hierarchy. A GTIN, or brand plus MPN, is the primary key, and "accurate matching to products can't be assured when GTIN is missing". **Each colour and size variant carries its own GTIN** ([Merchant Center: GTIN](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6324461)). Above that level, `item_group_id` ties variants into a style, and only colour, size, pattern, material, gender and age group may vary inside a group. Google's T-shirt example is 3 sizes × 3 colours = 9 products under one group ([Merchant Center: item_group_id](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6324507)). Two offers with the same GTIN but conflicting attributes get flagged ([Merchant Center 12473099](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/12473099)), so Google also checks attribute consistency. For the app, this gives three levels of identity: **exact** is style plus colourway, size is ignored for price comparison, and colour-only differences are **same style, other colour**. A difference in collar, sleeve length or garment type falls outside the group entirely, so it is a **different product**. That is the principle that should have rejected the round-neck tees for the collared Tommy Hilfiger shirt.

Lens takes two separate paths. Exact identification comes from identifiers it reads (barcode or OCR text matched to Shopping listings) or from the same catalogue item. For outfits, Google says Lens "recommends items that are stylistically similar" ([VentureBeat, 2018](https://venturebeat.com/ai/google-lens-now-recognizes-over-1-billion-products)). Google Research describes the product domain as needing to "distinguish very fine-grained details between similar-looking products from different brands". It built a dedicated universal *instance-level* embedding benchmark (UnED) because general category-level embeddings cannot do this ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/introducing-the-google-universal-image-embedding-challenge/); [arXiv 2309.01858](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.01858)). The lesson for a small app is to treat "same product" as a stricter test applied after retrieval, never as the top hit of a similarity list. Google publishes no end-to-end Lens shopping pipeline, so beyond these documented pieces, the claim that Lens works this way is inference.

The industrial systems agree on the shape of the cascade and differ only in scale. Alibaba's Pailitao predicts category first, then retrieves within it, then re-ranks on metadata. Category prediction reached 91% accuracy when a classifier was fused with a kNN vote, and identical-item recall@1 was only **46.5%** ([Alibaba, arXiv 2102.04674](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2102.04674)). Pinterest's Shop The Look puts its hard constraints in the infrastructure: separate per-category indexes and a gender-or-unisex filter. Restricting by category cut "Bad" top-5 results by **17.1%**. Colour, pattern and fabric go into training as auxiliary tasks, not filters. Pinterest's rubric grades a different colourway as "Similar", not "Extremely Similar" ([Pinterest, arXiv 2006.10866](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.10866)). eBay partitions by predicted category but searches competing partitions when the top prediction is uncertain ([eBay Tech](https://www.ebayinc.com/stories/blogs/tech/tips-for-visual-search-at-scale/)). Amazon's same-product model trains on **hard negatives from the same subcategory**, and lets the title steer attention over image regions ([Amazon Science](https://amazon.science/blog/using-computer-vision-to-weed-out-product-catalogue-errors)). Zalando's 2024 system is the closest analogue to this task. It blocks on fuzzy brand similarity, runs kNN, applies a distance threshold tuned on precision-recall curves, and sends the top 3 to expert validators. That lifts precision from 0.16–0.29 to **0.90–0.94**, while crowd workers could not reach 90% ([arXiv 2403.11593](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.11593v1)). For a small app, the cheap verifier replaces Zalando's experts.

## Title text beats pixels for the attributes that caused the failures

Category is easy. Neckline is the hardest attribute there is. Marqo-FashionSigLIP, an open Apache-2.0 model, scores **0.758 P@1** on category-to-product retrieval, against 0.690 for base SigLIP ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/Marqo/marqo-fashionSigLIP)). That is enough for a zero-shot "watch vs T-shirt vs slides" check to back up title keywords. Fine attributes read from images are much weaker. On DeepFashion-MultiModal, zero-shot **neckline F1 was 15% for GPT-4o-mini and 48% for Gemini 2.0 Flash**. Sleeve length scored about 60% and upper-body colour 58–69% ([arXiv 2507.09950](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09950v1)). A January 2026 study of nine VLMs, including GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro, found **70.8% F1** on fine attributes when the attribute is visible. Deciding whether it is visible at all scored only **34.1% NA-F1**, which the authors call the key bottleneck ([arXiv 2601.15711](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15711)). Text extraction is far stronger: GPT-4 extracts product attributes from titles at about **91% F1** ([arXiv 2403.02130](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.02130v1)).

That ordering sets the extraction rule. Read neckline, sleeve, garment type and gender first from the source title and the target's structured fields. Flipkart titles are templated from attributes and name the neck outright ("ADIDAS Solid Men Polo Neck Blue T-Shirt") ([Flipkart adidas](https://www.flipkart.com/x/p/itm2064871066e94?pid=TSHHJXCPFDZY65DU)). Myntra's page JSON carries `articleType`, gender and `articleAttributes` such as Neck and Sleeve ([Myntra PDP](https://www.myntra.com/38159155)). Use the image only to fill gaps, and ask a binary question ("fold-over collar with a button placket: yes / no / unclear") rather than a six-way neckline classification. Whether a binary question is easier is an inference; no published collar-specific accuracy exists. Every field must allow `unknown`. **A veto fires only on two confident, known values that conflict**, never on `unknown`, because VLMs over-commit on attributes they cannot see. The Tommy collared shirt is the textbook case. "Polo" or "collar" in its Amazon title against "Round Neck" in a Flipkart title, or Neck=Round on Myntra, is a known-versus-known conflict and a clean reject, and no image model has to judge a collar. Colour should be compared at family level (navy ≈ dark blue), because photography shifts colour and embeddings under-represent it. Myntra's own colour-variant research deliberately trains embeddings to be **invariant to colour** ([arXiv 2104.08581](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08581)).

Codes need the same discipline. A value counts as a style code only if it matches a per-brand pattern and contains no size token. Examples are Puma `^\d{6}\d{2}$` (528856 + colour 01), Nike `^[A-Z]{2}\d{4}-?\d{3}$` and Adidas `^[A-Z]{1,2}\d{4,5}$`. Amazon's own seller guidance warns that part and model numbers are often just the seller's SKU ([Amazowl](https://wiki.amazowl.com/sop/amazon-foundations/part-model-numbers)). Short all-caps tokens such as `SIGCYA` or `LGTGRE` are brand colour abbreviations. They belong in a colour field, mapped through a small per-brand dictionary (LGTGRE → light grey) or left as `unknown`, and are never a key. Keep two keys per listing: a full key (style plus colour) for exact matches and a base key (style only) for "same style, other colour". The patterns are working heuristics. Formats for Tommy Hilfiger, Van Heusen and other licensee brands have not been verified.

## Retrieval sets the ceiling; Lens is an optional second net

Nothing downstream can recover an item that retrieval missed. Name search on Indian marketplaces ranks the true item poorly. Code search does not work: Flipkart's search for "puma 528856" returned unrelated shoes, and Myntra's search for "HJ5228-101" returned watches and trackpants ([Flipkart search](https://www.flipkart.com/search?q=puma+528856); [Myntra search](https://www.myntra.com/hj5228-101?rawQuery=HJ5228-101)). What works is a brand-filtered search with several phrasings. On Myntra, "v5 rnr" with `f=Brand:Nike::Gender:men,men women::Color:White` cut 775 results to **3**, with the target at rank 2 ([Myntra filtered search](https://www.myntra.com/casual-shoes?f=Brand%3ANike%3A%3AGender%3Amen%2Cmen%20women%3A%3AColor%3AWhite&rawQuery=v5%20rnr)). The app's own fix for Flipkart's page-2 ranking applies the same principle. A brand filter shrinks the pool, and Flipkart card image filenames embed the brand code (`xl-52885699-puma-original-…`), which settles a candidate without opening its page. One trap remains: the page HTML also contains the image filenames of *recommended* products, so read the code only from the card's own image ([Flipkart Nike PDP](https://www.flipkart.com/nike-court-vision-lo-nn-sneakers-men/p/itmde6fa6fc67661?pid=SHOGRVJPSM9MFYYE)). Myntra paging is unreliable (`&p=2` repeated page-1 items), so rely on better queries and filters on page 1 rather than deeper pages. The query set should be three or four variants: the canonical or brand-site name plus colour, model line plus garment type, the marketplace's own attribute template ("tommy hilfiger men polo neck navy t-shirt"), and the bare model name.

Google Lens is the strongest general "find this garment elsewhere" signal, but only through third-party scrapers, and two 2025–26 changes limit it. Google **removed the Lens Products tab**, so price and store data now appear only on some `visual_matches` and `exact_matches` entries ([SerpApi](https://serpapi.com/google-lens-products-api); [SearchAPI.io](https://www.searchapi.io/docs/google-lens)). SerpApi also has an **open bug where `country` does not localise results**. With `country=IT`, results came back from Australia, Bahrain and the US ([GitHub #3317](https://github.com/serpapi/public-roadmap/issues/3317)). No source shows Lens APIs returning flipkart.com or myntra.com links for India. Treat Lens as an optional parallel recall step: send a garment-cropped Amazon image with `q=<brand>`, keep only flipkart.com and myntra.com links filtered in code, and send them through the same gates and verifier as everything else. SearchAPI.io costs **$40 for 10,000 searches** ($4 per 1,000) and returns price fields on matches ([SearchAPI.io pricing](https://www.searchapi.io/pricing)). SerpApi costs $75 for 5,000 ([SerpApi pricing](https://serpapi.com/pricing)). All of these scrape Google against its terms. Google sued SerpApi in December 2025, and the core DMCA claims were reportedly dismissed in July 2026 ([IPWatchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2025/12/26/google-sues-serpapi-parasitic-scraping-circumvention-protection-measures/); [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/google-loses-dmca-bid-to-treat-search-scraping-like-dvd-piracy/)). Put the provider behind an interface so it can be swapped. The terms-clean option is Cloud Vision Web Detection at $3.50 per 1,000, which returns pages reusing the same catalogue image but no prices ([Cloud Vision pricing](https://cloud.google.com/vision/pricing)). The Bing Visual Search API was **retired on 11 August 2025** ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/lifecycle/announcements/bing-search-api-retirement)). TinEye only finds near-duplicate image copies. Google's own Vision Product Search requires you to index your own catalogue and is reportedly in maintenance mode, so it does not fit.

## The pipeline, stage by stage

The design runs once per source listing per target platform. The source listing is an Amazon.in child ASIN collapsed to colour level, because Myntra's style id is one colour at one MRP, with sizes underneath ([Fynd Myntra attributes](https://documentation.fynd.com/commerce/docs/openapi/listings-management/myntra/attribute-myntra)).

| Stage | What it does | Rule | Cost per lookup |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Parse source | Read brand, category, gender, colour, Style Number/MPN, Model Name, MRP, manufacturer/packer, images from the Amazon child ASIN | Code accepted only if it passes the brand pattern and has no size token; colour abbreviations routed to colour | 1 page fetch |
| 1. Attributes | Build typed JSON: category, neck/collar, sleeve, gender, colour family, pattern, team/player/season for jerseys. Title regex first, then structured fields, then image (FashionSigLIP zero-shot for category; batched VLM only for missing fields) | Every field allows `unknown`; cache per listing | ≈$0 locally; ≈$0.0003 if a VLM fills gaps |
| 2. Retrieve | 3–4 query variants per platform with brand (and gender/colour) filters, page 1; optional Lens with domain filter. Classify every fetch as OK / EMPTY / BLOCKED | BLOCKED is reported as "check failed", never "not listed" | 3–8 searches; +$0.004 with Lens |
| 3. Gate | Drop candidates whose **category, brand (alias-normalised), gender** or pack count conflicts; then known-vs-known conflicts on **collar, sleeve**, colour family; jersey team/season/kit | Code full-key equal → exact; base-key equal, colour differs → other colour; both valid codes differ → reject | Free |
| 4. Re-rank | FashionSigLIP max cosine over all image pairs, plus name-token overlap, MRP equality, manufacturer/packer string equality | Keep top 3–5 above a calibrated floor | ~70 ms/image on CPU |
| 5. Verify | One listwise VLM call: anchor plus ≤5 labelled candidates, titles under images, per-attribute checklist, then verdict `exact / same_style_other_colour / similar / none` with per-field `unclear` | Run in two candidate orders, require agreement; escalate disagreements to a stronger model | ≈$0.0005 per call (Haiku 5.5) |
| 6. Decide | Combine code, attributes, corroboration and verdict into a tier | See below | Free |

Stages 0–3 replace the special cases. The Tommy watch and the Adidas slides fail stage 3's category gate, because the source title says "T-shirt" or "polo" and the candidate's category says watch or flip-flops. The Van Heusen and Bewakoof listings fail the brand gate when brand is read from Myntra's `brand.name` field and compared through an alias table (for example "Tommy Hilfiger" = "TOMMY HILFIGER" = "Tommy Jeans" only if the brand family policy says so). Seller SKUs never become keys. The gate's one subtlety is eBay's lesson: when the source category is genuinely ambiguous (a "polo T-shirt" that some sites file as T-shirts), map polo and tee to adjacent categories and let the neck attribute decide, rather than dropping the candidate.

The verifier prompt follows the evidence. Selecting among a list with a "none" option beat independent pairwise yes/no by an average of **16 F1 points** in LLM entity matching. The same study found accuracy falls as the true match sits lower in the list ([Wang et al., arXiv 2405.16884](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.16884v3)), so shuffle candidate order and require two orderings to agree. Label images "Anchor:", "Candidate 1:" and so on, as Claude's vision guidance recommends for multi-image prompts. Send images of at least 200 px on the short side, since smaller images invite hallucination ([Claude vision docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision)). Ask for the comparison table before the verdict, covering category, collar/neckline, sleeve, placket, logo position and size, print or graphic text, colour, and brand text. Run at temperature 0, which beat temperature 1 by 6–7 F1 points in attribute extraction ([arXiv 2507.09950](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09950v1)). Following Zalando's validators, hide noisy fields such as price and size from the visual judgement. For jerseys and graphic tees, the decisive tokens are player name, number, sponsor and slogan, and the prompt should ask for them to be quoted. Treat VLM accuracy as unproven. No public benchmark measures VLM same-product precision on apparel images. General VLMs "struggle to understand… real-world e-commerce product images" ([Amazon VIT-Pro, NAACL 2025](https://preview.aclanthology.org/moar-dois/2025.naacl-industry.57.pdf)), and on plain basics they tend to call two black crew-neck tees the same. **Let the VLM veto freely, but never accept on its word alone.**

The decision tiers follow from this. **Exact** needs either a full-key code match, or all of the following together: the VLM returns exact in both orderings, no attribute conflict, no `unclear` on collar or graphic, brand agrees, and at least one non-visual corroboration. That corroboration can be a canonical-name match, equal MRP plus a matching manufacturer or packer string, or reuse of the same catalogue image. The supply-chain strings are copied verbatim across sites. On the Puma test case, the Amazon and Myntra manufacturer strings differed only in whitespace ([Myntra 35720668](https://www.myntra.com/tshirts/puma/x/35720668/buy); [Amazon.in B0FGJ6FVZ6](https://www.amazon.in/dp/B0FGJ6FVZ6)). They confirm a match but cannot separate colour siblings from the same factory, which is why colour and the image still matter. **Same style, other colour** means base-key equality, or name and supply-chain agreement with a colour mismatch; return the correct sibling if the platform lists one. **Similar** covers candidates that pass the gates but fail strict verification, plus ties the VLM cannot break. Myntra really does keep twins: two white Nike V5 RNR styles share name, MRP, manufacturer and seller and differ only in images ([Myntra 38159155](https://www.myntra.com/38159155); [38159174](https://www.myntra.com/38159174)). **Not listed** is allowed only when every fetch returned OK and every candidate failed a gate. A blocked fetch yields "check failed". Myntra listings with no code land in "exact" only through the evidence route above. Otherwise they become "similar — needs confirmation", which matches what the mock run surfaced.

Costs are dominated by fetches, not models. Claude Haiku 5.5 is $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens. The Batch API halves that ([Claude pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). A listwise call with six 512-px images, about 800 prompt tokens and 300 output tokens costs roughly **$0.0005**, so two orderings across two platforms come to about $0.002 per lookup. A Sonnet 5.5 escalation is about $0.009 per call ($2 / $10 per million). Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite at $0.25 / $1.50 is a comparable alternative ([Google AI pricing](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)). These per-call figures are arithmetic from list prices, not measured bills. FashionSigLIP runs locally at a reported ~70 ms per image on CPU, from a third-party listing whose other details conflict with Marqo's, so measure it yourself ([Mixpeek](https://www.mixpeek.com/model/Marqo/marqo-fashionSigLIP)). At 1,000 lookups a month, models cost a few dollars, and Lens adds $4–15 depending on the vendor. The real costs are engineering time and the terms-of-service risk of fetching Flipkart and Myntra, whose terms prohibit automated access ([Myntra Terms](https://www.myntra.com/termsofuse)). Keep lookups user-triggered, low-volume and cached.

## Build the gates and the label set before any model

Build in order of failures prevented per hour of work. First come the typed parsers and gates (stages 0, 1 text-only, and 3): category keywords, brand alias table, gender, code validation with size-token rejection, colour-abbreviation dictionary, and the known-vs-known neck and sleeve veto. Together they cover the watch, slides, round-neck, Van Heusen, Bewakoof, SKU and LGTGRE failures with no model at all. Second, generalise the Flipkart fix into a retrieval module: brand-filtered multi-query search, card-image code extraction, and OK/EMPTY/BLOCKED status, so "not listed" becomes trustworthy. Third, add the listwise VLM verifier and the four tiers, which turns unconfirmed Myntra matches from errors into honest "similar" outputs. FashionSigLIP re-ranking comes fourth; with brand-filtered pools of 10–50, it mainly controls VLM cost and the position of the true item in the list. Lens comes last, behind a feature flag, after a pilot of 20–50 Amazon images on free tiers measures how often Flipkart or Myntra links actually come back. That hit rate is the largest open unknown in this design.

Evaluate the parts separately, because their failures differ. Measure **retrieval recall@K** on its own: is the true item anywhere in the candidate pool? Matching quality cannot exceed it. Measure the matcher on labelled pairs: **200–500 Amazon→target pairs**, balanced across exact matches, same-style-other-colour pairs, same-brand look-alikes (polo vs tee, the Tommy case), and cross-category traps (watch, slides). Hard negatives from the same subcategory are what taught Amazon's model fine distinctions ([Amazon Science](https://amazon.science/blog/using-computer-vision-to-weed-out-product-catalogue-errors)). Label quality matters more than volume. Pinterest's raters reached 95% consistency with an expert edge-case guide, against 47% on MTurk, and dataset work moved quality more than model changes did ([arXiv 2006.10866](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.10866)). So write a one-page labelling guide that defines the four tiers before labelling anything. Set the **auto-accept rule for "exact" to at least 95%, ideally 98%, precision** on that set. A 2026 production cascade calibrated its auto-accept stage to 98% precision and escalated the rest, accepting 68–77% coverage ([arXiv 2608.25037](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25037)). Report AUCPR rather than F1, because F1 hides the high-precision region ([arXiv 2403.11593](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.11593v1)). Report coverage per platform and per brand tier: brand-direct sportswear, licensee brands such as Tommy Hilfiger and Van Heusen, and private labels. Keep the 10-T-shirt mock run as a regression suite that must pass on every change. Log every user "not the same" click as a new hard negative. Once a few thousand code-confirmed pairs accumulate, train a Zalando-style linear projection head on frozen embeddings. It is the cheapest model upgrade on record: AUCPR rose from **27 to 57** with a head trained in about ten minutes on one GPU ([arXiv 2403.11593](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.11593v1)).

## Conclusion

The mock-run failures look like seven separate bugs, but they come from one design gap. The app let similarity decide identity. Google, Pinterest, Alibaba, eBay and Zalando all treat similarity as a way to propose candidates and decide identity with something stricter: identifiers, structural gates, or a verifier with the right to say "none". Seen that way, the rule-per-case approach was already the start of the gates layer, just not written as general typed constraints. Once it is, adding a new brand or category means adding a dictionary entry, not new code.

Two things remain unproven, and they should shape the roadmap. No public benchmark shows how accurately a VLM separates a collared from a round-neck tee or one colourway from another on Indian marketplace photos. That makes the local labelled set the most valuable asset in the system, more valuable than any model choice. And because published apparel coverage tops out around half, the four-tier output is the product, not a fallback. An app that says "same style, other colour" or "similar, needs confirmation" when it is unsure will earn more trust than one that is right 90% of the time and confidently wrong the rest.
