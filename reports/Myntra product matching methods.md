# Match Myntra by evidence, not by code

Nothing public on Myntra carries a brand style code. Matching an Amazon.in listing such as Puma `52885601` or Nike `HJ5228-101` to the exact Myntra product therefore cannot be a lookup. It is evidence accumulation over a small candidate set. Myntra's product pages expose no brand code, vendor article number or EAN: not in the page JSON, the JSON-LD (whose `sku` and `mpn` are both Myntra's own style id), the meta tags or the image filenames. Searching Myntra for a style code returns watches and trackpants ([Myntra PDP 38159155](https://www.myntra.com/38159155); [Myntra search HJ5228-101](https://www.myntra.com/hj5228-101?rawQuery=HJ5228-101)). Myntra's own published research, most of it from 2017–2021, deals with image deduplication, colour-variant clustering and "similar products". None of it covers exact matching against other retailers. Commercial trackers and vendors do not solve this either. Consumer tools promise "the same product (or a close match)", and the most candid vendor puts apparel match rates at **40–60%** ([PriceHistory](https://pricehistoryapp.com/myntra-price-history); [DataWeave](https://dataweave.com/blog/from-mess-to-match-solving-product-identity-at-retail-scale)). The design that works is a precision-first cascade. It resolves the brand code to the brand's canonical product name, searches Myntra with that name plus `Brand/Gender/Color` filters, and gates candidates on article type, gender, colour and MRP. It then confirms with the supply-chain strings (manufacturer, packer, importer) and brand description copy that Myntra and Amazon both copy word for word, and uses images only to tell apart colourways that are otherwise identical. Tested live on two cases, this gives a clean **exact match** for the Puma tee (Myntra 35720668). For the Nike V5 RNR it gives a correct **"Possible match"**, because Myntra lists two white men's styles (38159155 and 38159174) whose structured data is identical and which only a picture can tell apart.

## Myntra's research optimises for "similar", never for "same elsewhere"

Myntra's publications fall into two groups: work on the shape of its own catalogue, and work on recommendation. Its tech site lists the style-deduplication paper, a domain-adaptation patent, colour-variant identification, "Discovering Similar Products", "One Embedding to do them all", ShopLook and progressive attribute extraction. It lists no post on seller-listing dedup or competitor matching ([tech.myntra.com](https://tech.myntra.com/)).

The deduplication work comes closest to cross-store matching. Banerjee et al. say outright that products "are catalogued differently in terms of background, model poses and lighting between the two different e-commerce platforms". Their fix is to crop the garment, extract **VGG-16 4096-d features**, and pass them through an autoencoder trained with an adversarial domain classifier (gradient reversal). Duplicates are then found by KNN. The adaptation cut KL divergence between domains from **1.36 to 0.075**. However, the evaluation compared Myntra catalogue images with GAN-generated designs, not with another retailer, and it reports precision-recall curves but no threshold ([Myntra dedup PDF](https://tech.myntra.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/System_for_Deduplication_of_Machine_Generated_Designs_from_Fashion_Catalog.pdf)). The patent version, US20200285888A1, makes the same cross-platform framing and does not describe matching against a competitor ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20200285888A1/en)).

Two other papers carry a warning for exact matching. Myntra's colour-variant work defines variants as products that "match exactly in their design… but only to differ in their color", and it trains embeddings that are deliberately **invariant to colour jitter**. A ResNet34 with part-based contrastive learning (PBCNet), followed by Ward clustering, beat a supervised triplet baseline (CScore 0.796 vs 0.466 on one kurta set) ([arXiv 2104.08581](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08581); [arXiv 2112.02910](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.02910)). A design embedding of this kind is excellent at finding a product's colour family and poor at picking the exact colourway, so colour has to be checked separately. "Discovering Similar Products" partitions everything by article type before computing similarity, using either attribute vectors or an NPMI co-browse graph (≥5 co-browses, NPMI ≥0.15) ([SIGIR eCom 2017](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2311/paper_19.pdf)). "One Embedding" blends image and text embeddings, with weights of image 0.1 and text 0.9 for clicked-purchased similarity ([arXiv 1906.12120](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1906.12120)). Three lessons carry over to a matcher: gate hard on article type and gender, crop before embedding, and do not let an embedding decide colour. No Myntra source publishes a cosine threshold for "same product", so any threshold has to be calibrated locally.

Integrator documentation gives the clearest view of Myntra's identity model. Sellers must supply a **vendorArticleNumber** (up to 40 characters) and a **styleGroupId**, defined as "all sizes of a product with same MRP and colour". A colour-variant group id is optional, and so, explicitly, is **GTIN** ([Fynd: Myntra attribute guidelines](https://documentation.fynd.com/commerce/docs/openapi/listings-management/myntra/attribute-myntra)). A public Myntra style id is therefore one colour at one MRP, with sizes as SKUs underneath. Amazon's colour × size child ASINs should be collapsed to colour level before matching, and MRP is part of the identity, not just a price. The vendorArticleNumber is probably the brand code, but it stays in the seller back end and never reaches the public page.

## Trackers and vendors match by title search and admit low apparel coverage

No consumer tool documents an identifier-level match for Myntra. PriceHistory keys on the Myntra URL. Its cross-store button shows "the same product (or a close match)" on Ajio and Nykaa and describes no method ([PriceHistory](https://pricehistoryapp.com/myntra-price-history)). Buyhatke (one million users) advertises "Lookalike" to "find exact products across stores" and "similar products at a lower cost" in the same listing ([Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/buyhatke/ojplmecpdpgccookcobabopnaifgidhf?hl=en)). Earlier coverage says it "will automatically search for the same product on every shopping portal", which points to title search rather than codes ([Digit](https://www.digit.in/features/general/shop-online-smarter-with-the-compare-hatke-browser-extension-20245.html)). Third-party Myntra APIs fetch by Myntra style id or keyword, never by brand article number ([Anysite](https://docs.anysite.io/api-reference/myntra/myntraproductssearch); [Scrapingdog](https://docs.scrapingdog.com/myntra-scraper-api/myntra-product-api)). A GitHub repository search for Myntra matching or price comparison returned nothing usable: only zero-star deal bots and Amazon–Flipkart keyword comparators.

Vendors describe one common pipeline: identifiers where present, LLM attribute extraction, text and image embeddings (CLIP, Siamese, CNN) for retrieval, and human review for edge cases. DataWeave's June 2026 write-up gives the only numbers with a stated split. Apparel match rates "typically sit between 40% and 60%" against ~95% for electronics, precision exceeds 85%, and about 90% of decisions are automated ([DataWeave](https://dataweave.com/blog/from-mess-to-match-solving-product-identity-at-retail-scale)). Actowiz claims "99%+" for Myntra/Ajio/Meesho/Amazon variant mapping on "Example Data" with no test set ([Actowiz](https://www.actowizsolutions.com/product-variant-mapping-myntra-ajio-meesho-amazon-retail-intelligence.php)). Intelligence Node's 99% likewise comes without a dataset ([Intelligence Node](https://www.intelligencenode.com/blog/product-matching-ai-for-ecommerce/)). A Zilliz case study of a large Indian retailer describes 1024-d image+description embeddings, pre-filtered by category, brand and price, then thresholded and manually reviewed, over ~5M SKUs per competitor. It gives no precision figure ([Zilliz](https://zilliz.com/customers/indian-online-retailer)). In short, the best-funded systems still find a confident match for only about half of apparel and send the rest to people. A small system should treat "Possible match" as a normal, frequent output, not a failure.

## The public Myntra page is rich in everything except the brand code

A Myntra product page is server-rendered HTML. It carries `window.__myx.pdpData`, a JSON object that a plain GET returns without login, and a bare `https://www.myntra.com/<styleId>` resolves to the full page. The fields that matter for matching ([Puma fixture 35720668](file:///Users/sid/scruge/tests/fixtures/my_puma_tad_tee_pdp.html); [Nike 38159155](https://www.myntra.com/38159155)):

| pdpData field | Example (Puma 35720668) | Role in matching |
|---|---|---|
| `name`, `brand.name` | "Puma Train All Day Men Breathable Training Tee" | Compare with the brand's canonical name |
| `analytics.articleType / gender / subCategory` | Tshirts / Men / Topwear | Hard gate |
| `baseColour`, `colours[]` (sibling styleId + label) | Black; 7 siblings incl. "Yellow / Black", "Grey / Black" | Colour gate; whole colour family in one fetch (null for Nike) |
| `mrp`, `price` | 1799 / 719 | MRP equality is part of Myntra's style identity |
| `manufacturer`, `sizeSellerData[].packerInfo / importerInfo`, `countryOfOrigin` | "Aryan Apparels… IMT Manesar", "PUMA Sports India Pvt Ltd., 496, Krrish Towers…", India | Strong same-product corroboration |
| `productDetails` description | "PRODUCT STORY… Designed for active comfort…" | Brand copy shared verbatim with Amazon |
| `articleAttributes` (~34 keys) | Fabric Polyester, Neck Round, Sleeve Short | Soft checks; materials can split twins |
| `sellers[]` | 6206 "Puma Sports India Pvt. Ltd.(NSCM)" | Brand-direct vs reseller |
| `catalogAttributes.catalogDate` | 16 Jul 2025, season fall 2025 | Separates re-listings of one name |
| `media.albums[].images[]` | assets.myntassets.com templates | Visual tie-break |

Two of these fields agree across sites almost character for character. Amazon B0FGJ6FVZ6 lists the manufacturer as "Aryan Apparels Private Limited Plot no 266 Sector 06 IMT Manesar 122051", which differs from Myntra's only in whitespace and a trailing ", India". Its packer string matches Myntra's `packerInfo` exactly, and its description opens with the same "Product Story" text ([Amazon fixture](file:///Users/sid/scruge/tests/fixtures/amz_puma_tee.json); [Amazon.in B0FGJ6FVZ6](https://www.amazon.in/dp/B0FGJ6FVZ6)). These signals confirm a match but do not discriminate between candidates: every colour sibling from the same factory shares them. The long number in older image filenames (`…-490175379406-1.jpg`) is not a brand code either. After a "490" prefix its digits repeat the upload timestamp embedded in the same filename (`1753794067116`).

Search behaves as name search with a query parser on top. `https://www.myntra.com/<slug>?rawQuery=<query>` embeds `searchData.results.products[]`, 50 per page, with productId, name, brand, `primaryColour`, colour hexes, `colourVariants[]`, MRP, sizes, catalogDate, articleType and up to eight view images. The `f=` parameter adds filters (`f=Brand:Nike::Gender:men,men women::Color:White`) ([Myntra search](https://www.myntra.com/nike-v5-rnr-mens-shoes?rawQuery=Nike%20V5%20RNR%20Men's%20Shoes)). Live tests showed both the method's power and how fragile it is:

| Query | Results | Rank of target |
|---|---|---|
| "Puma Train All Day Men Breathable Training Tee" | 408 | **1** (35720668), siblings at 2–8 ([live](https://www.myntra.com/puma-train-all-day-men-breathable-training-tee?rawQuery=Puma%20Train%20All%20Day%20Men%20Breathable%20Training%20Tee)) |
| "puma-train-all-day-tee" (shortened) | 106 | Swamped by other "Train All Day Essentials" tees |
| "nike v5 rnr" | 775 | First V5 at rank 38: "rnr" was parsed as Sport=running ([live](https://www.myntra.com/nike-v5-rnr?rawQuery=nike%20v5%20rnr)) |
| "Nike V5 RNR Men's Shoes" | 27 | 38159155 at **4**, all V5 RNR men's colourways in 1–7 |
| "v5 rnr" + `f=Brand:Nike::Gender:men,men women::Color:White` | **3** | 38159155 at 2, between 47153224 and twin 38159174 ([live](https://www.myntra.com/casual-shoes?f=Brand%3ANike%3A%3AGender%3Amen%2Cmen%20women%3A%3AColor%3AWhite&rawQuery=v5%20rnr)) |
| "HJ5228-101" | 664,304 | Absent; watches and trackpants on top |

Paging is unreliable. `&p=2` repeated page-1 items and changed `totalCount` from 408 to 340, so a good query plus filters, kept to one page, beats deep pagination. A bare brand number in the URL path is a trap. Myntra routes `/52885601` to its product handler and returns `pdpData: null`, while `/528856` resolves to unrelated Blackberrys trousers ([Myntra /52885601](https://www.myntra.com/52885601); [Myntra /528856](https://www.myntra.com/528856)). The JSON gateway that scrapers cite, `/gateway/v2/product/{id}` and `/gateway/v2/search/…`, returned **HTTP 401** to a plain GET. Probing stopped there, because getting past it would mean circumventing access control ([gateway](https://www.myntra.com/gateway/v2/product/38159155)).

Images resize on the fly. Putting a transform before `/v1/`, as in `https://assets.myntassets.com/w_300,q_80/v1/assets/images/…`, returns a 300×400 JPEG of 7–10 KB. That works for both the older id-and-slug paths and the newer date-and-token paths ([example](https://assets.myntassets.com/w_300,q_80/v1/assets/images/2025/NOVEMBER/21/YQGeAg33_ff91ea391f9047c4acc0278b1ecafa84.jpg)). For the Puma tee, Myntra uses its **own photo shoot** (a different model on grey). Amazon and Flipkart use Puma's global studio images ([Amazon image](https://m.media-amazon.com/images/I/519awlkUeCL._SL400_.jpg); [Myntra image](https://assets.myntassets.com/h_400,q_80,w_300/v1/assets/images/35720668/2025/7/29/9195d851-5bc8-4868-aa84-5fb9e98150fc1753794067116-Puma-Train-All-Day-Mens-Breathable-Training-Tee-490175379406-1.jpg)). Perceptual hashing between Amazon and Myntra therefore fails for apparel, and the visual check has to compare products, not pixels.

The legal position is lopsided. robots.txt starts `Allow: /`, disallows paths such as `*/filters*`, `*/size-chart/*` and `/proxy/apify/*`, and does not block product or slug-search pages ([robots.txt](https://www.myntra.com/robots.txt)). The Terms of Use, however, prohibit any "page-scrape, robot, spider or other automatic device… to access, acquire, copy or monitor any portion of the Platform" ([Myntra Terms of Use](https://www.myntra.com/termsofuse)). Fourteen test GETs met no bot check, but that is no permission. Lookups should be user-triggered, low-volume and cached, and the product owner has to accept the ToS risk knowingly.

## A six-stage cascade that knows when to say "Possible match"

The design treats each Amazon child ASIN, collapsed to colour level, as one query, and Myntra style ids as the target unit. Each stage either narrows the candidate set or adds independent evidence. The cost per listing is 1–2 brand-site requests (cached by code), 1–3 Myntra search pages, at most 5 product-page fetches, a few thumbnails, and at most 3 vision-model calls.

| Stage | Action | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Parse Amazon | Read Style Number / MPN and validate against the brand pattern (Puma `^\d{6}\d{2}$`, Nike `^[A-Z]{2}\d{4}-\d{3}$`); also brand, gender, product type, colour, M.R.P., manufacturer, packer, importer, country of origin, description, Model Name, main image | A code with a size token or failing the pattern is treated as absent |
| 2. Bridge to a name | Look the base code up on the brand site (in.puma.com resolves `528856` to "Train All Day Men's Breathable Training Tee", swatch 01 = PUMA Black). If blocked (Nike, adidas, H&M returned 403), build the name from Amazon Model Name, title and description, optionally LLM-rewritten into Myntra's "Brand + Name + Gender + Type" style, and flag it as **unconfirmed** | Never put a code or abbreviation ("RNR", "TAD") in the query |
| 3. Search Myntra | `/<slug>?rawQuery=<full name>&f=Brand:<b>::Gender:<g>::Color:<c>`, plus one unfiltered variant; page 1 only; parse `searchData` | Classify each fetch as OK / EMPTY / BLOCKED; BLOCKED means "check failed", never "not on Myntra" |
| 4. Gate | Keep candidates with equal brand, gender, articleType and pack count, and a compatible colour (compare Amazon colour with `primaryColour` and the sibling label, so "Black" does not pass "Grey / Black") | Zero survivors → **Not found** |
| 5. Corroborate | Fetch top ≤5 product pages; score name similarity to the canonical name, MRP equality, normalised manufacturer/packer/importer equality, description-copy overlap, country of origin, brand-direct seller, materials | Add `colours[]` siblings to the candidate set so a wrong-colour hit is converted, not lost |
| 6. Visual tie-break | Only when ≥2 candidates are still tied: fetch `w_300` thumbnails, crop the product, compare to the Amazon image with an embedding plus an explicit colour/detail check, then a vision model asked to name concrete differences (swoosh colour, logo, panels) | The vision model may veto freely; it may break a tie only if it names a visible difference and rejects the rival |

The decision has five outcomes, and the line between the top two is where precision is won or lost.

**Exact match** requires all of the following. Brand, gender and article type agree. Colour agrees. The Myntra name matches the brand-confirmed canonical name, or the description copy matches Amazon's. MRP is equal. At least one supply-chain string matches after normalisation. Finally, the candidate is **unique**: no other survivor shares its name, colour and MRP, or the visual stage separated the twins on a named, visible difference and the vision model returned DIFFERENT for every rival.

**Possible match** is the answer whenever the evidence points at a product family but cannot prove which member it is. That covers several situations:

- two or more candidates stay tied after stage 5 and the image check is UNSURE or unavailable
- the name came from an LLM rewrite rather than the brand site, with no supply-chain or description corroboration
- MRP differs, which under Myntra's styleGroupId definition can mean a different style or a re-listing
- the colour agrees only at base level for a multi-tone product
- the only Myntra seller is a reseller rather than the brand

The result then lists all tied candidates with the field that failed to separate them. **Colour variant** means the name and supply-chain evidence match but the colour does not, and returns the correct sibling from `colours[]` if there is one. **Not found** means search succeeded but every candidate failed a gate. **Check failed** covers blocked fetches. Every outcome ships with its evidence trail: matched fields, vetoed candidates and the reason for each.

The two live cases exercise both sides of the line. For **Puma 52885601**, the brand site confirms the name and "PUMA Black". The full-name query ranks 35720668 first, and it is the only Black in its eight-member colour family ("Yellow / Black" and "Grey / Black" fail a label-level colour check). Manufacturer, packer, description and country of origin all match Amazon. MRP ₹1,799 matches Puma's site, and the seller is Puma Sports India. The result is **Exact match: 35720668**, with no image needed. The trap in this family lies elsewhere: a Blue query would find two ids with the same name and colour, 35720667 and 44935702 ([Myntra search](https://www.myntra.com/puma-train-all-day-breathable-training-tee)), which would force the tie-break or a Possible match.

For **Nike HJ5228-101**, nike.com/in blocked plain HTTP, so the name "Nike V5 RNR Men's Shoes" is unconfirmed by the brand. The filtered search returns three white men's candidates. Two of them, 38159155 and 38159174, share name, MRP ₹7,995, manufacturer (PT Sumber Masanda Jaya, Indonesia), importer, seller and search hex colours, and differ only in images (all-white/silver against white with a black Swoosh), a "Mesh" versus "Textile" materials attribute, and a few seconds of catalogue date ([38159155](https://www.myntra.com/38159155); [38159174](https://www.myntra.com/38159174)). Myntra exposes no `colours[]` list for Nike to help. Which twin is HJ5228-101 was **not established** in testing, because the Amazon image for that code was not fetched. Until a vision check against Amazon's image names a decisive difference, the correct output is **Possible match: 38159155 or 38159174**. That is the system working as designed, not failing.

## Conclusion

On Myntra, the bottleneck for exact matching is not the similarity model. Myntra deliberately strips the brand code from public pages, and it sometimes keeps two live style ids for what is visibly the same name, colour and price. The most valuable signals are therefore unglamorous ones: the brand's canonical name, MRP, and supply-chain strings that sellers copy verbatim because Indian labelling law requires them. Images matter only at the very end, to separate near-identical twins, and Myntra's own colour-invariant research explains why an off-the-shelf embedding cannot do that alone.

These tests do not settle several things, and they should be measured before any threshold is trusted. The tests cover two products. The brand-site bridge is verified only for Puma. Whether name-plus-filter search reaches the top five across brands, and how often twins like the Nike pair occur, both need a labelled set of a few hundred Amazon→Myntra pairs, scored separately for brand-direct sportswear, licensee brands and private labels. Expect Puma-like brands to land mostly in Exact match, and plain basics and resellers mostly in Possible or Not found. That split is consistent with the 40–60% apparel coverage the industry reports, and it is the honest shape of the product.
